Project plan · MaleCNS v1.0 · September 2026

Chess for the Digital Fly

A living fruit fly will never play chess. Its wiring diagram can be made to try. This plan turns the complete male fly connectome into a trainable neural network with the fly's exact neurons, connections and signs, shows it the board through its own eyes, reads its move off the neurons that command its body, and measures how far that wiring can go.

6phases, about 14 weeks part-time
166,691neurons in the connectome
25.6 Mconnections, all kept
1rented GPU for training

The honest version

A real fly can't do this. A fruit fly learns simple associations over minutes: this smell goes with a shock, this visual pattern goes with heat. Each eye gives it roughly 750 image points. There is no route from that to perceiving 64 squares holding 12 kinds of piece, let alone planning a move. The nearest real-fly experiment is described at the end, and it is a performance piece, not chess.

What can be done is to train the digital fly. The connectome gives us every neuron, every connection between them, and, from the dataset's neurotransmitter predictions, whether each connection excites or inhibits. What it does not give us is the strength of each connection. So we keep all the anatomy fixed and learn only the strengths, then ask whether that wiring can support chess.

This is not a fantasy: two 2024 papers in Nature did exactly this kind of modelling on fly connectomes. Shiu and colleagues simulated the whole female brain as a spiking network with synapse counts as weights and reproduced real feeding and grooming circuits. Lappalainen and colleagues built a connectome-constrained network of the visual system, trained it on a task, and found it predicted how real neurons respond. We follow their recipe on a bigger connectome with a stranger task.

What we will be able to say at the end: either "the male fly's brain wiring, with learned synaptic strengths, plays chess at this rating, and these regions do the work", or the equally publishable negative: "the wiring can't carry it, and here is exactly where it fails."

The rules of the game

These constraints are what make the claim honest. Loosen any of them and it stops being the fly.

Fixed by the connectome

  • The set of neurons: every traced brain neuron
  • The set of connections: 25.6 M edges, none added
  • The sign of each neuron's output (acetylcholine excites; GABA and glutamate inhibit)
  • The starting strength: the counted synapses

Learned

  • A non-negative gain on each existing connection
  • A bias and a time constant per cell type
  • The linear readout from descending neurons to moves

Not allowed

  • New neurons or new connections
  • Flipping a sign
  • Input anywhere except the photoreceptors
  • Output from anywhere except the descending neurons

Controls that must run alongside

  • Same neurons, connections randomly rewired with matched degrees
  • Same wiring, signs shuffled
  • A plain dense network of similar parameter count

Build the digital fly

done · 14 Sep 2026

A runnable, differentiable simulation of the brain, wired exactly as in MaleCNS v1.0, that behaves like a fly before it has learned anything.

Result. 144,209 neurons and 21.27 million signed edges, built from the public weight and neurotransmitter tables. A graded model with each neuron's inputs normalised and a global gain of 1.5 passes both checks: sugar on the labellum drives the feeding motor neuron MN9 above rest with a z-score of 4.8 against 20 random sensory controls, and light on the right eye drives the right eye's lamina, T4/T5 and lobula plate while the left stays silent. The activity of both experiments plays back in the Cloud Atlas. Two honest caveats: the motor response is small (about 1% of the peak) because input normalisation under-weights strong inputs onto neurons with thousands of synapses, which learned gains in Phase 3 will fix; and the Mac's GPU has no sparse-matrix support, so the forward pass runs on CPU at 0.7 s per tick for a batch of 128, confirming that training needs a rented CUDA GPU. Details in flybrain/README.md.

  1. Fetch the tables. From the public bucket: the neuron-to-neuron weights (traced-only file, 508 MB), the per-neuron neurotransmitter predictions (43 MB), and the annotations you already have.
  2. Pick the neuron set. Traced neurons on the brain side: optic lobe, central brain, visual projection, descending and ascending neurons, brain sensory axons. Leave the nerve cord out of version one. Roughly 130,000 neurons and 20 million edges.
  3. Assemble the weight matrix. Sparse, post × pre. Entry = synapse count × the sign of the presynaptic neuron's predicted transmitter. Dopamine, serotonin and octopamine neurons are modulatory, not fast; set them to zero in version one and revisit in Phase 3's fallbacks.
  4. Choose the neuron model. A rate unit with leak: r ← r + (dt/τ)(−r + softplus(W·r + b + I)), dt = 5 ms, τ about 20 ms, 32 ticks per move. Rates rather than spikes so the whole thing is differentiable.
  5. Implement it. PyTorch, compressed-sparse-row matrix multiply, batched over positions, backpropagation through time, gradient clipping. Scale the matrix at initialisation so the untrained network neither dies out nor explodes.
  6. Biology check, before any chess. Stimulate the sugar-sensing taste neurons: the proboscis motor neurons in the gnathal ganglion should respond, the test Shiu and colleagues used. Stimulate one eye: motion-sensitive cells in the lobula plate should respond. If either fails, the matrix is assembled wrong.
  7. Wire it into the atlas. An activity mode in the Cloud Atlas: the 140,024 cell bodies glow by firing rate, tick by tick. The positions and body IDs are already in the viewer's data.
Done whenA forward pass over 130,000 neurons runs at batch size 128 in well under a second on a GPU, both biology checks pass, and activity renders in the atlas.

Show it the board

done · 15 Sep 2026

The board reaches the visual system through the fly's own eye, and the visual system provably represents all of it.

Result, 15 Sep 2026: target met. The plan asks for more than 99.5% of squares read from the medulla and lobula by a linear readout. The generic readout, principal components of the whole stage, gives 99.98% on random placements and 100.0% on realistic positions (kept 176 components, partial scaling, penalty 1e-08); the retinotopic readout, each square read from the neurons of its own eye columns, gives 100.0% / 100.0%. Of the two levers, the training set was the one that mattered: at 3,072 boards the generic readout reached 91.6%, at 7,168 boards 99.98%, with the probe's component floor, scaling and penalty chosen on the validation split. The receptor-rate cap made no difference (0.8, 0.5 and 0.3 gave the same retinotopic accuracy on a pilot) and stays at 0.8. The stimulus itself reads at 100.0%, the lamina at 100.0% and the visual projection neurons at 99.98%: the whole board leaves the optic lobe intact. It then thins out in the untrained central brain (66.9%) and reaches the descending neurons at 50.1% (63.5% realistic), the baseline Phase 3 must beat.

  1. Use the real eye map. The annotation table's assignedOlHex1 and assignedOlHex2 columns give every optic-lobe column its hexagonal position on the eye: 892 columns in the right eye. Photoreceptors carry no coordinates, so each is placed by its strongest strictly one-per-column partner. The board is a retinotopic grid, 8 rank bands by 8 files of equal column count, over the 645 columns that have both colour receptors traced: 10 columns per square. The original 3×3-facet patch was not possible, because only 380 columns have traced R1–R6 cells.
  2. Encode the pieces as light. The eye cannot resolve glyph shapes at ten columns per square, so pieces are told apart by colour: each square's 13-way state is a point on a 4×4 grid of levels on the R7 and R8 colour channels (empty at the origin, white pieces along the R7 edge, black along the R8 edge). Side to move is a global brightness bias on R1–R6.
  3. Inject as photoreceptor currents into the 2,239 right-eye photoreceptor axons, 2.0 × level. A per-square classifier on a square's own photoreceptors recovers the state at 100%, so the encoding has no ceiling below the target.
  4. Probe it. Train a linear readout from medulla and lobula activity at the final tick to reconstruct all 64 squares. With untrained gains it must recover the board at better than 99.5% square accuracy. Then probe the descending neurons the same way to record how much of the board reaches them before any training.
Done whenThe optic lobe provably carries the whole board. Nothing after this phase is worth running until it does.

Teach it the rules and the taste of a good move

in progress · first GPU run 15 Sep 2026

Given a board, the descending neurons produce a legal and plausible move.

Result so far, 15 Sep 2026. One rented RTX 4090 trained the per-edge variant (27.1 million parameters, every synapse free to change its strength) on 768,000 Lichess positions in 125 minutes for about a dollar. On held-out positions the fly's moves are legal 68.0% of the time (best 72.1%), match the human move 20.3% of the time (best 22.5%) and land in the top three 39.5% (best 42.2%); chance is 0.7%, 3% and 9%. The Mac control that may only rescale neurons, not synapses, reaches 17% legal and 6% top-1: the freedom at the synapse is what learns. The targets of 99% legal and 35% top-1 are not reached; the loss was still falling when the run ended, so more training is the first lever.

  1. Data. The Lichess open database: at least 10 million positions from games rated 1600 to 2200, plus Stockfish best moves at depth 10 for a 1-million-position subset. Parse and check legality with python-chess.
  2. Readout. One linear layer from the 1,314 descending-neuron rates to 4,096 from-square × to-square logits plus 4 promotion logits. Train it unmasked so legality has to be learned; mask to legal moves only when playing.
  3. Loss and optimiser. Cross-entropy on the target move, plus an auxiliary value head on the same rates predicting win, draw or loss. AdamW, clipped gradients, 32 ticks. Expect 2 to 10 hours per 10-million-position epoch on one RTX 4090-class GPU, and 5 to 10 epochs.
  4. Curriculum. First legality alone: predict any legal move, target above 99% legal without the mask. Then imitation of the engine's best move: target top-1 at or above 35% and top-3 at or above 60% on held-out positions. Small conventional networks trained to imitate humans reach about 50% top-1, so 35% under the fly's constraints would be a strong result.
  5. Run the three controls from the rules of the game with identical data and compute. This table is the scientific content of the project; keep it from day one.
Done whenAbove 99% legal moves, top-1 at or above 35% on held-out positions, and the controls table is filled in.

Make it play

weeks 7–10

A rated chess engine whose entire policy is the fly's brain.

  1. Wrap it as a UCI engine so any chess program can run it: a python-chess UCI loop, one forward pass per move, about 10 ms on a GPU or around a second on your Mac's CPU.
  2. Fine-tune by playing. Reinforcement learning against Stockfish with its strength limited, climbing from Elo 1350 to 1600 to 1800: REINFORCE with the value head as baseline, or DAgger using Stockfish's move as the correction. Keep imitation loss mixed in so it does not forget legality.
  3. Rate it properly. cutechess-cli, 200-game matches against limited-strength Stockfish and against a random mover, Elo with error bars from ordo. Targets: 100% against random, at or above 1200 against Stockfish-limited. Honest expectation: somewhere between 1000 and 1500. Below 1000 means the wiring constraint binds, which is a finding, not a failure.
Done whenAn engine binary exists with a measured rating and error bars.
5

Look inside

weeks 11–12

Find out which parts of the fly's brain the chess lives in, and whether the anatomy or the training did the work.

  1. Ablate by region. Silence each of the 108 neuropils in turn and re-measure move accuracy and rating. Render the losses as a heat map in the Cloud Atlas: the mushroom body, the central complex and the lateral horn are the interesting candidates.
  2. Decode across time. At each tick, which regions carry the from-square, the to-square, and whether the king is in check? Where is the chosen move first readable?
  3. Compare gains to anatomy. If the learned gains stay close to 1, the counted synapses did most of the work. If they diverge wildly, the network rewired itself within the constraint. Either is worth reporting.
Done whenAn ablation map, a decoding timeline and a gains-versus-anatomy figure exist.
6

Ship the demo

weeks 13–14

Anyone can play the fly in a browser, watch it think, and watch it move the pieces itself.

  1. The page. A chessboard with a 3D model of the fly beside it. On each move the fly walks from its resting spot to the piece, carries it to its destination square (a captured piece is carried off the board first), and walks back to exactly where it started. The Cloud Atlas next to the board shows the brain activity that produced the move; a sidebar lists the candidate moves and the most active regions. A rigged low-poly fly with a walk cycle in three.js, its path generated from the engine's chosen move.
  2. Inference. About 20 million multiply-adds per tick, 32 ticks, so roughly 1.3 billion operations per move: a small backend, or WebGPU in the browser.
  3. Write it up. The rules of the game, the controls table, the rating, the ablation map. That write-up is the real product.
Done whenA stranger can play the fly online and see its brain light up.

Risks and fallbacks

  • The constraint is too tight and the fly never beats random.First per-edge gains plus per-type biases. Then let the dopamine, serotonin and octopamine connections act as learned multiplicative gates, which is literally how the fly's mushroom body learns. Last resort, allow a small percentage of new edges and report it as a deviation from the rules.
  • A 130,000-unit recurrent network explodes during training.Scale the matrix's spectral radius below 1 at initialisation, clip gradients, shorten to 16 ticks, swap softplus for tanh.
  • The eye can't resolve the board.750 columns are enough for 64 squares with glyphs, but empty versus occupied must be robust. The Phase 2 probe gates everything after it; if it fails, enlarge each square to 4×4 facets and use both eyes.
  • Compute creeps past the budget.Fewer positions, 16 ticks, mixed precision, and a cheaper first pass with one gain per pair of cell types (about 11,000 types) instead of one per edge (20 million).
  • "That's not a real fly."Every reader will say it. Pre-empt it with the rules of the game, the biology checks in Phase 1, and the controls table from Phase 3. The claim is about the wiring, and the wiring is real.

The living-fly option

If a real fly has to be involved, the nearest honest experiment is a fly in the loop. A tethered fly walks on an air-supported ball inside a closed-loop virtual-reality arena. The engine proposes two candidate moves as two visual patterns, left and right; the fly's turning picks one. Operant conditioning with infrared heat, the classic flight-simulator paradigm, can teach a fly to prefer a pattern within an hour or two.

What you get is a fly that reliably picks the cued pattern, which is the engine's move, not a fly that plays chess. It needs a fly lab and roughly $10,000 to $30,000 of rig. Worth doing only as a performance piece, and only after the digital fly has a rating to compare it to.
